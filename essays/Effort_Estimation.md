## Effort Estimation and Tracking Reflection

### How did you make your effort estimates?

Our team made effort estimates by combining gut feeling, breaking tasks into subtasks, drawing on past experience, and having short team discussions. At the start of each phase, we would break the project down into individual pieces, such as the frontend UI, backend API, and database work, and then assign rough hour ranges to each one. These ranges were mostly based on intuition and whatever experience each team member had with similar tasks from past projects or assignments.

We did not use any formal historical data since this was a class project and we had no prior logs to reference. Instead, historical data just meant personal memory, recalling roughly how long something similar had taken before. When estimates differed between teammates, we would talk it through and agree on a number. The process was casual and conversational rather than structured.

### Even though your estimates were often off, did estimating in advance provide any benefit?

Even though our estimates were not always accurate, the act of estimating still had some value. Going through the process forced us to think about the full scope of work before starting, which helped us catch things we might have forgotten, like setting up deployment or writing tests. Without that planning step, those tasks could have snuck up on us at the end.

One concrete benefit was team alignment. When everyone estimated independently and then compared, disagreements sometimes revealed different assumptions about what a feature actually included. For example, one teammate estimated the search feature at two hours while another said six. That gap led to a useful conversation about whether filters and pagination were in scope. Even if the final number we agreed on was still off, that discussion was worthwhile.

### Was tracking actual effort useful?

In our case, tracking actual effort did not provide much direct benefit during the project. Because we did not log time consistently and instead relied on memory after the fact, the data was not precise enough to confidently inform decisions. Most of our project choices were driven by deadlines and whether features were done, not by effort numbers.

That said, reflecting on actual effort at the end gave us a better intuition going forward. Knowing that a task we estimated at three hours actually took about one hour helped recalibrate our sense of scale for future work. The takeaways were more qualitative than quantitative, things like realizing UI work tends to go faster than we expect.

### How did you track your actual effort?

We tracked effort informally, relying mostly on memory at the end of each week or project phase. There was no dedicated time-tracking tool involved. Each team member would mentally reconstruct how much time they spent, sometimes using commit timestamps or file modification dates to help anchor their memory to specific days.

I would estimate this tracking was about 60 to 70 percent accurate at best. Without logging time in the moment, it is easy to forget short sessions, undercount time lost to distractions or context switching, or confuse time spent thinking about a problem with time actually spent working on it. Reconstructing effort from memory also tends to produce round numbers, which suggests the estimates were more approximate than precise.

### Reflection: what would you change about your estimation or tracking process next time?

The biggest change I would make is tracking effort in real time instead of reconstructing it later from memory. Even something simple, like starting a timer when sitting down to work and stopping it when finished, would make the data much more reliable. A lightweight tool like Toggl or even a shared spreadsheet with daily entries would be more than enough.

I would also try to break tasks down more carefully at the start. Our decomposition was useful but sometimes too broad, grouping things like the entire backend into a single task. Finer breakdowns make both estimating and tracking more meaningful, and they make it easier to spot where a particular area is taking longer than expected.

### AI use

No AI tools were used for estimating or tracking effort during this project. All estimates came from team discussion and individual judgment, and all tracking was done manually through memory and retrospective reconstruction.
